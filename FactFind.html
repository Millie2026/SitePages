What is a fact find?

The first step in addressing concerns — an informal meeting to gather all relevant facts before any decision is made. It is not a disciplinary hearing.

Why does it matter?

Failure to follow up concerns could lead to evidence being destroyed and potential tribunal claims. Consistent process protects everyone involved.

When to use it

Potential disciplinary matter, conflict between colleagues, allegations of bullying or harassment, complaint from a service user, or a safeguarding concern.

Key principle: The purpose of a fact find is to discover all relevant facts in a fair, reasonable and objective manner. Minor issues do not always require a full investigation — but all fact finds must be documented in writing with expectations set going forward.

Before conducting a fact find or investigation, a written plan helps you stay focused and objective. It does not need to be lengthy — it needs to be clear.

Your plan should cover

  • Outline of the issue or concern
  • Policies and procedures to review
  • Issues that need to be explored or clarified
  • Sources of evidence (care notes, PASS, MAR charts, logs)
  • Persons to be interviewed and proposed order
  • Date, time, location and notetaker arrangements
  • Any reasonable adjustments needed
  • Rota changes if required
  • Persons to supply their own statement

Who can conduct a fact find?

A fact find can be conducted by any appropriate person who has no prior involvement in the concern — it does not have to be the individual's line manager.

If the concern involves the line manager, a more senior or independent person should be used.

The investigator must be fair, objective and impartial — avoid anyone with a perceived conflict of interest.

✓ Open questions

Encourage free recall. Start with Why, How, What, Describe. These give you the most information.

"Can you tell me about the care call you attended on Friday?"

○ Closed questions

Used for confirmation — usually a yes or no. Use to pin down specific facts once you have the full account.

"Did you attend the care call on Friday?"

✗ Questions to avoid

Leading, suggestive or multiple questions can invalidate your findings and be challenged later.

"You didn't give the medication, did you?"
Questions for each stage of the meeting
Opening the meeting
Can you tell me in your own words what happened on [date]?
Is there anything you'd like to tell me before we start?
Do you understand this is a fact find, not a disciplinary?
Are you happy to proceed, or do you need a short break?
Exploring the concern
What happened next? Walk me through the sequence of events.
Who else was present? What did they say or do?
What impact do you think your actions had?
Is there anything else relevant I haven't asked about?
Probing & mitigating factors
Were you aware of the relevant policy at the time?
Is there anything that affected your judgement that day?
Has anything like this happened before, to your knowledge?
What would you do differently in the same situation?
Summarising & closing
Let me summarise the main points — is that accurate?
Is there anything you'd like to add before we finish?
Are you happy to review the notes and confirm accuracy?
Is there anything affecting your wellbeing I should know?
When interviewing witnesses
Do
Allow the witness to refer to documents to refresh their memory
Pause or adjourn if the witness becomes very upset
Record all pertinent facts accurately and thoroughly
Remind the witness to keep the matter confidential
Be open to answering queries the witness has beforehand
Don't
Encourage the witness to offer their personal opinion
Offer your own opinion or speculate on the outcome
Draw hasty conclusions before all facts are gathered
Disclose confidential information from other interviews
Be afraid to question the witness's version of events
⚖️

Consistent process. Not identical outcomes.

Two people can go through exactly the same process and reach different outcomes — and that is right. People are different, circumstances are different, and the facts will never be exactly the same. What must be consistent is how you conduct the fact find, how you gather evidence, and how you record your decision. The outcome itself is not black and white.

Factors that influence the outcome
📅

Length of service & history

A first concern in four years of service is very different to a repeated pattern of the same behaviour.

🤝

Acceptance of responsibility

Whether the individual acknowledges what happened and shows insight matters when deciding next steps.

💛

Mitigating circumstances

Personal pressures, health, staffing levels, or factors outside the individual's control may reduce the severity of the response.

🔁

Pattern or isolated incident

A single lapse in judgement is different to a pattern. Has a similar concern been raised before, even informally?

👥

Impact on others

The effect on service users, colleagues, or the organisation is a key factor in deciding whether informal resolution is appropriate.

📚

Training & awareness

Was the individual trained and aware of the relevant policy? Lack of awareness points toward support rather than sanction.

What this means in practice

🔄 Process must be the same

Every fact find follows the same steps — plan it, document it, give the individual the opportunity to respond, review the evidence, record your reasoning.

⚡ Outcomes can differ

The same type of concern in two different sets of circumstances may genuinely warrant different responses. That is not inconsistency — it is fair and proportionate decision-making.

✓ The record is what matters

Document the facts, the factors you considered, and why you reached your conclusion. A well-reasoned decision can always be defended. An undocumented one cannot.
